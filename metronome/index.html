Tempo Markings and BPM: Largo to Presto

Classical scores rarely write a number — they write a word. Here is roughly what those words mean in beats per minute, so you can set the slider to match what the page asks for:

  • Largo — about 40–60 BPM. Slow and broad, the tempo of a hymn or a lament.
  • Adagio — about 66–76 BPM. Slow, but with direction; typical of a lyrical slow movement.
  • Andante — about 76–108 BPM. Walking pace, and the most comfortable tempo to sing or practice at.
  • Moderato — about 108–120 BPM. Moderate; marches and straightforward pop land around here.
  • Allegro — about 120–168 BPM. Fast and bright, the tempo of most upbeat songs.
  • Presto — about 168–200 BPM. Very fast, with prestissimo above that.

The slider covers 40–240 BPM, which spans everything from a slow practice tempo to a fast reel, so you can dial in any of the markings above — or simply the number a teacher gave you.

How to Practice with a Metronome

A metronome is not a speedometer, it is a coach. Used well it stops you from practicing your mistakes at speed:

  • Start slower than you think. Set a tempo at which you can play the passage perfectly, even if that is half the performance speed.
  • Only speed up when a run is clean three times in a row. If it falls apart, drop back rather than repeating the mess.
  • Subdivide at slow tempos. Count two clicks per beat for eighth notes, or four for sixteenths, so the pulse stays obvious.
  • Practice the joins, not the whole piece. Loop the two bars that trip you up at a steady click, then put them back in context.
  • Use it for scales and warm-ups. Five minutes of scales in time beats twenty minutes of drifting.
  • Turn it off sometimes. Playing without a click is the skill you are actually building; the metronome is scaffolding.

Tempos for Guitar, Piano, Drums and More

  • Guitar — 60–80 BPM for chord changes, 100–120 BPM for strumming patterns. A click is the fastest way to cure a rushing strum.
  • Piano — about 50–70 BPM hands separately, then bring the hands together at the same click before raising the tempo.
  • Drums — 70–90 BPM for groove work, then push higher for double-time feels. Counting out loud against the click keeps fills steady.
  • Violin and strings — long-tone and bowing exercises at around 60 BPM, one bow stroke per click.
  • Singing — andante, roughly 76–108 BPM, matches a comfortable walking pulse for breath control and diction.
  • Running and cardio — many runners aim for about 170–180 steps per minute, so set the slider in that range and take one step per click to even out your cadence.

Why Musicians Practice with a Click

A metronome removes the one thing you cannot hear while you are playing: your own drift. Players tend to speed up in easy passages and slow down in hard ones, and because the whole performance drifts together it sounds fine from the inside. A steady click exposes it immediately.

The second reason is ensemble work. If you rehearse alone at the tempo your group will use, you arrive already knowing the tempo instead of guessing it from the first bar. The third is measurable progress: when a passage that was clean at 80 BPM is now clean at 100 BPM, you have evidence that the practice worked.

A browser metronome is also more convenient than a hardware one. There are no batteries to replace and no box to lose, and it opens on the phone that is already sitting on your music stand.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I use the metronome?

Drag the BPM slider to your tempo (40–240), click Start, and the metronome ticks at that speed. Click Stop to pause.

What is a good BPM to practice at?

Start at a tempo where you can play the passage cleanly, even if that is much slower than the performance tempo — often 50-70 BPM for a beginner, or half speed for a difficult run. Raise the slider by 5 or 10 BPM only after three clean repetitions.

What does BPM mean?

Beats per minute — the number of clicks you hear in one minute. A higher BPM is a faster tempo. Most pop songs sit between 100 and 130 BPM, and this metronome covers 40 to 240.

What tempo is andante or allegro?

Roughly 76-108 BPM for andante, which is walking pace; 108-120 for moderato; 120-168 for allegro; and 168-200 for presto. Drag the slider to the number and you are playing at that marking.

Does the metronome work without an internet connection?

Once the page has loaded, yes. The click is generated in your browser, so the metronome keeps running even if you lose the connection. There is nothing to download and no account to create.

Can runners use a metronome for cadence?

Yes. Many runners aim for about 170-180 steps per minute, so set the slider in that range and take one step per click. It is a simple way to check and even out your cadence, and it works for walk-run intervals as well.
