How to Use the What to Eat Wheel

The what to eat wheel is a free spinner that picks tonight's dinner from your own list: type the meals you have in mind, press Spin, and the wheel stops on one at random — backed by the browser's cryptographically secure random number generator, so the pick is fair every time. No sign-up, nothing to install, and your list stays on your own device.

Type your meal ideas into the rows, then press Spin in the middle of the wheel. The wheel lands on a random dish and shows it as tonight’s pick. + Add gives you another row, the Text tab takes a comma separated list in one go, and Import accepts a .txt or .csv. Add "Order takeout" or "Cook at home" as options if you like.

Set Up Your Own Dinner List

Start from whatever your household actually eats and type those dishes in as rows. Ten to fifteen meals is plenty — enough variety to feel like a real choice, few enough that the wheel stays readable.

Mix quick weeknight meals with one or two treats so the result stays realistic, and add takeaway as an entry for the nights nobody wants to cook.

Meal Ideas by Cuisine, Mood and Budget

  • Fast and cheap — pasta, omelette, noodles, beans on toast, fried rice
  • Something warming — curry, stew, soup, chilli, roast vegetables
  • Weekend cooking — pizza from scratch, a slow braise, a big breakfast
  • No cooking tonight — takeaway, delivery, or a picnic of whatever is in the fridge

Building the list once saves the same discussion every evening.

Save Your Food Lists for Later

Press + to add a list and the current meals are saved in your browser automatically as you type, ready for the next time the question comes up. You can hold separate lists for weekday dinners, weekend cooking and birthdays, and switch between them by clicking the tabs without retyping anything.

Saved lists live on your own device, so removing them means clearing them here — nothing is copied to a server.

Settling the Dinner Debate

Repeating the same question at the end of a long day is tiring. A spin removes the negotiation and hands the decision to chance, which most households accept far more readily than one person's suggestion.

If the result is genuinely unappealing, spin once more — but the first reaction to a result usually says more than the debate did.

From One Meal to a Whole Week

The same list can plan further ahead than a single dinner. Spin for tonight, press Remove & spin again to take that dish out, then spin again for tomorrow — a few presses lay out a week of meals with no repeats.

Households that shop once a week find it quicker to spin the plan first and write the shopping list from the results.

What to Eat Tonight: Ideas for Every Situation

Busy weeknights

On weeknights the real constraint is time. Load the wheel with meals you can genuinely cook on a Tuesday — stir-fry, omelette, pasta, sheet-pan chicken, fried rice — plus one or two takeaway options for the late nights. If the wheel lands on something that needs an hour you do not have, spin again; a list that respects your schedule is a list you will keep using.

Families with picky eaters

Let everyone nominate two meals each and spin the combined list. The wheel turns "I don't want that" into a shared result nobody has to argue with, and because the wheel visibly stops on its own, the outcome feels fair to kids and adults alike. Add one Friday treat slot so the list never feels like a chore.

Couples and roommates

Two people, two opinions, one decision — the classic deadlock. The wheel removes the "I don't mind, you pick" loop entirely. Agree the list in advance while everyone is calm, then let the spin make the call. If both of you groan at the result, that is useful information: update the list.

Students and shared houses

In a shared house the food wheel doubles as a budget tool: fill it with cheap, cookable staples and one takeaway option, and let the spin set the weekly rotation. Pair it with the random group generator to split who cooks and who cleans up afterwards.

House Rules That Keep the Spin Fun

A few simple conventions stop the wheel from turning into another argument. The most popular is the three-spin rule: whatever lands on the second or third spin stands, and the debate ends there. Others add a single veto token per person per week, or a "loser cooks" clause when the result is takeaway. A shared consequence — such as whoever vetoed the first result doing the dishes — keeps everyone honest about which vetoes really matter.

Whatever you choose, agree it before the first spin. Rules invented after an unwelcome result tend to be less popular with the household.

Why Spinning Works Better Than Asking

Deciding what to eat is a low-stakes question with surprisingly high friction: it arrives at the end of the day, when decision fatigue is at its worst, and every option on the table is acceptable — which is exactly why choosing one feels impossible. Psychologists call it choice paralysis, and it gets worse the more similar the options are.

Handing the decision to a random spin sidesteps the whole comparison. Nobody has to defend a preference, nobody's suggestion gets shot down, and the small drama of the wheel slowing down gives the household something to react to together. The meal was always going to be fine; the wheel just ends the discussion.

Beyond Dinner: Lunches, Snacks and Meal Prep

The same wheel works anywhere food decisions repeat. Swap in sandwich fillings and lunch spots for the weekday lunch question, snack options for the after-school debate, or breakfast ideas for slow weekend mornings. Meal-preppers spin five dinners on Sunday, pin the list to the fridge, and shop for exactly those ingredients — one spin session becomes the whole week's plan. Potlucks work too: add the dishes you are considering bringing and let the wheel pick your contribution. You can also keep a separate wheel for takeaway nights only, so the treat stays a treat.

Tips for a Better Food Wheel

  • Keep entries specific. "Tacos" beats "Mexican food" — a concrete dish is an answer, a cuisine is another debate.
  • Mix effort levels. Include 15-minute meals alongside weekend projects so the wheel stays realistic on tired days.
  • Refresh monthly. Retire the meals everyone is tired of and add one or two new dishes — a stale list gets ignored.
  • Match the season. Salads and barbecue in summer; stews and curries in winter. Save seasonal lists separately and switch between them.
  • Use 📋 Copy. Paste the winning meal straight into the household chat so there is a record of the verdict.

Small tweaks like these keep the wheel doing its one job well: ending the dinner debate quickly, with a result everyone can live with.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I use the what to eat wheel?

Add the meals you’re considering (comma separated), click "Spin", and the wheel stops on a random dish. You can remove it and spin again until something sounds good.

Is the what to eat wheel free?

Yes. The what to eat wheel is 100% free, runs in your browser, and never requires a sign-up or download.

How many meals should I add to the wheel?

Around ten to fifteen works best. A shorter list spins quickly, keeps every dish visible on the wheel and still feels like a real choice.

Can I save my dinner ideas for next time?

Yes. Your list is saved in your browser automatically as you type, and you can load it again with one click. Press + to add another list and switch between them with the tabs — weekday meals, weekend cooking, and so on.

Can I use the wheel for lunch or breakfast?

Absolutely. Swap the entries for sandwich fillings, lunch options, breakfast ideas or a whole week of meal planning — the wheel does not mind what the entries are.

What if the wheel picks a meal we do not fancy?

Spin once more, or press Remove & spin again to drop that dish before spinning again. You can also edit the list to retire a meal the household has grown tired of.

Can the wheel plan more than one meal?

Yes. Spin for the first meal, press Remove & spin again to take it out, then spin again. Repeating that builds a run of different meals for the week.

Can I use the wheel if the household has dietary restrictions or allergies?

Yes. Keep a separate list per need — vegetarian, gluten-free, no-dairy — and switch between them with the tabs. The wheel only ever picks from the meals you put on it, so nothing lands that the household cannot eat.

Can I share my meal list with the rest of the household?

Yes. Press 🔗 Share to copy a link that carries the current list, and send it to whoever needs to weigh in. Opening the link loads the same meals on their device.

Can I add takeaway or delivery nights to the wheel?

Yes. Add entries like Order takeout or Cook at home just as you would any meal — they get the same fair slice as everything else. Many households keep one takeaway slot a week so the treat stays a treat.

Does the what to eat wheel work on a phone?

Yes. It runs in any modern browser and adapts to a phone or tablet screen, so you can spin on the sofa without a laptop. Your lists stay on that device.
